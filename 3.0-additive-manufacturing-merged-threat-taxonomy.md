## Summary

Additive manufacturing (AM) moves the value of a part into the data that produces it. Two published taxonomies describe how that data and the surrounding supply chain can be attacked: Yampolskiy et al. (2018) and Kumar et al. (2025). Neither relates its threats to a regulation. This release merges the two into one taxonomy of **72 nodes** (5 attack goals, 24 attack targets in 5 families, 29 attack methods in 9 families) and adds three annotations to every node:

1. the **security property** it affects (confidentiality, integrity, availability),
2. the **requirement of the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)**, Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, that it engages, with a strength label derived from one stated rule, and
3. for every attack method, the **MITRE ATT&CK mitigations** that address it, with their application to AM.

The intended use is the cybersecurity risk assessment that Article 13(2) to (4) CRA requires of a manufacturer. A manufacturer of AM software, firmware or connected equipment walks from an asset to the methods that reach it and the goals they serve, and copies the annotations into a threat record (Section D).

The taxonomy is an analytical instrument. It is not legal advice, it does not establish that a product complies with the CRA, and it has not yet been validated by practitioners (Section 10).

## A. Scope and intended use

**Subject.** Attacks on the AM digital thread and the supply chain around it: design files, intermediate models, tool paths, process parameters, sensor and quality data, AM software and firmware, AM equipment, feedstock, logistics, the manufactured part and the rights attached to it.

**Meta-characteristic.** The ways in which the AM digital thread can be attacked such that confidentiality, integrity or availability is affected. Every dimension of the taxonomy serves this purpose.

**Three dimensions.** A threat is described by what is attacked (target), how (method) and to what end (goal). Goals, targets and methods combine many to many. The node hierarchy in Sections 1 to 3 defines the concepts. It does not by itself assert that a particular combination is supported by the sources. Supported combinations are listed separately (Section 4).

**Audience.** Manufacturers of AM products who must write a CRA risk assessment, users of AM products who want to state procurement requirements, and researchers who need a common vocabulary for AM threats under the CRA.

**Out of scope.** Likelihood and severity scoring, product-specific risk scenarios, and legal conclusions about a particular product.

## B. Sources and construction

### B.1 Sources

| Role | Source | Use in this release |
|---|---|---|
| Source taxonomy | **Y**: Yampolskiy et al. (2018), *Security of additive manufacturing: Attack taxonomy and survey* | Two sub-taxonomies (theft of technical data, AM sabotage), each with targets and methods. Supplies the AM-specific depth: geometry, required properties, process parameters, post-processing, equipment compromise, environmental impact. |
| Source taxonomy | **K**: Kumar et al. (2025), *Security of cyber-physical Additive Manufacturing supply chain: Survey, attack taxonomy and solutions* | One taxonomy with three dimensions (goals, targets, methods) across the whole supply chain. Supplies the overall frame and the supply-chain breadth: unauthorised production, restricted parts, overproduction, logistics. |
| Regulation | Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 (Cyber Resilience Act) | Source of the requirement annotation (Annex I, with the articles that make it operative). |
| Mitigation catalogue | MITRE ATT&CK, Enterprise and ICS mitigations | Source of mitigation names, identifiers and descriptions. |
| Supporting literature | Ruohonen, Kang and Ramadan (2025), *An Alignment Between the CRA's Essential Requirements and the ATT&CK's Mitigations* | Supports the general rationale for relating ATT&CK mitigations to CRA essential requirements. It is not a source of taxonomy nodes, of mitigation identifiers or of the AM-specific mapping in this release. |

Full references are in Section 12.

### B.2 Construction method

The taxonomy was built with the iterative method of Nickerson et al. (2013), starting from the meta-characteristic in Section A.

1. **Conceptual to empirical.** The three dimensions of K were adopted as the frame, because a risk assessment must state what is attacked, how and to what end, and because separating the three lets one method serve several goals.
2. **Empirical to conceptual.** Every target and method of Y was placed in the frame. Y's AM-specific detail was kept wherever it added a mechanism the frame did not have.
3. **Conceptual to empirical.** Every leaf was checked against the source taxonomies and the labelling was adjusted. The source papers were then read again, and the section references, the relationship figures and the path register of this release were verified against them.

### B.3 Merge rules

1. Two source nodes are **merged** only when they describe the same mechanism or asset at different levels of abstraction.
2. Source nodes that describe different mechanisms are **kept apart**, even when their outcomes coincide.
3. **No leaf introduces a threat that is absent from both sources.** Families are groupings added by the merge. Some leaves split one source node by artefact or stage, and some recast a source concept on another axis. These are marked (B.4).

Section 6 lists what was merged and what was kept distinct.

### B.4 Derivation of each node

Every node carries a **source trace** that names the source nodes it derives from, by section number of the source paper, and a derivation type:

| Derivation | Meaning | Nodes |
|---|---|---|
| `merged` | A node of Y and a node of K describe the same thing and were combined. | 16 |
| `kept` | Taken from one source. The other source has no equivalent node, or only a related passage. | 31 |
| `split` | One source node was divided by artefact or by stage, using content the sources state. | 7 |
| `recast` | Source content was moved to another axis, for example a manipulated element in Y that is listed here as a target. | 4 |
| `grouping` | Family node added by the merge to organise leaves. | 14 |

Section numbers in the source traces refer to the editions listed in Section 12. Where a paper uses a named paragraph instead of a numbered subsection, the paragraph name is given in brackets. The mapping of the source nodes that take part in a relationship is in `source-node-mappings.csv` (C.1).

### C.2 CIA labels

- **`[C]` Confidentiality:** unauthorised access to, disclosure of, or reconstruction of AM-related information, designs, process data or protected knowledge.
- **`[I]` Integrity:** unauthorised modification, corruption, substitution or falsification of AM data, processes, equipment or produced parts.
- **`[A]` Availability:** disruption, delay, degradation, denial or loss of access to AM systems, equipment, production stages or supply-chain operations.

A label records the property a node affects directly, not the worst case it could lead to. A node may carry more than one label.

### C.3 CRA mapping and strength rule

**What is mapped.** Each node is linked to the points of Annex I that it engages, and to the articles that make those points operative. Annex I, Part I, point (2)(a) to (m) lists product properties. Annex I, Part II, points (1) to (8) list vulnerability-handling duties. Article 13(1) is the operative duty for Part I and Article 13(8) for Part II. Notation: `Annex I.I(2)(f)` is Annex I, Part I, point (2)(f). `Annex I.II(7)` is Annex I, Part II, point (7).

**Unit of assessment.** The CRA regulates products with digital elements, not files, parts or materials. A design file, a tool path or a printed part is reached only through a product that stores, transmits or processes it. The strength label therefore answers one question: *does the mechanism of this node operate on or through an AM product, and does Annex I oblige the manufacturer of that product to counter it?*

An **AM product** in this document is a product with digital elements (Article 3(1)) in the AM digital thread: design and build-preparation software, slicers, workflow platforms, machine-control software, firmware, connected AM, post-processing and inspection equipment, components of these placed on the market separately, and remote data processing developed under the manufacturer's responsibility (Article 3(2)). General-purpose IT such as operating systems or logistics software is regulated in its own right and is not treated as an AM product here.

**The four labels.**

| Label | Definition | What the manufacturer writes in the risk assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | The mechanism of the node, as defined, always operates on data, commands, programs or configuration held by an AM product, or on an AM product itself, and Annex I addresses that mechanism. | The requirement applies. State how it is implemented. |
| **Conditional** | The mechanism operates on or through an AM product in some implementations only. The condition is stated. | Decide whether the condition holds for the product. If it does, treat the node as Direct. If it does not, the node is outside. |
| **Partial** | The mechanism, or the harm that defines the node, is physical, material, legal or commercial. An AM product ordinarily stands next to it, and Annex I requirements on that product prevent or reveal a stated part of the threat. | Address the reached part. Record that the rest lies outside the CRA and is handled by other means. |
| **Mostly outside CRA** | The mechanism does not operate on an AM product, and no Annex I requirement counters it. | Nothing under this node. Record any digital step that enables the threat under the node of that step. |

**Decision procedure.** The label of every goal and leaf was derived by asking three questions in order.

1. *Mechanism.* Does the mechanism of the node operate on data, commands, programs or configuration held by an AM product, or on an AM product itself? If always, go to question 2. If only in some implementations, the label is **Conditional**. If never, go to question 3.
2. *Coverage.* Do the engaged Annex I requirements address the mechanism itself? If yes, the label is **Direct**. If they address only controls around a harm that is defined in legal or commercial terms, the label is **Partial**.
3. *Proximity.* Does an AM product ordinarily stand next to the mechanism, so that its Annex I properties prevent or reveal part of the threat? If yes, the label is **Partial** and the part is named. If no, the label is **Mostly outside CRA**.

**Families.** A family carries the label of its leaves when they all agree. When they differ, the family is **Conditional** and the crosswalk lists the leaves by label. A family label is never used in a threat record. Records use leaves.

**Boundary rule.** A node is classified by its own mechanism, not by the steps that enable it or the consequences that follow. Counterfeiting is outside. The theft that made it possible is Direct. A defective mechanical part is outside. The file manipulation that produced it is Direct.

**Result for this release.** Of the 58 goals and leaves, 24 are Direct, 21 Conditional, 10 Partial and 3 Mostly outside CRA. Section 5 gives the label, the condition and the limits for every node.

### C.4 General limits that apply to every label

A label is a statement about a node. A threat record is a statement about a product. When a label is copied into a record, the following limits apply whatever the label says.

1. **Name the product.** The record states which product with digital elements stores, transmits, processes or executes the asset. If none does in the concrete case, the record is outside the CRA.
2. **Market.** The obligations of Article 13 attach when a product is placed on the market (Articles 3(21) and 3(22)). A tool built and used in-house is outside.
3. **User authorisation.** Annex I.I(2)(f) protects against manipulation or modification that the user has not authorised, and Article 3(42) defines active exploitation by reference to the system owner's permission. An attack by the product's own user, such as a licensee who alters a counter on its own printer, is outside.
4. **Non-digital copies.** The same information on paper or in a person's memory is outside.
5. **Who is obliged.** The requirement falls on the manufacturer of the product engaged, not on the company that operates it.
6. **Appropriate to the risks.** A label says that a requirement is engaged. It does not say what level of protection is appropriate (Annex I.I(1)), and it does not say that a product meets the requirement.

The mapping is an analytical reading of the regulation by engineers. Only a conformity assessment and, ultimately, the Court of Justice of the European Union can settle what the CRA requires of a given product.

### C.5 MITRE ATT&CK mitigations

Every method leaf carries a mitigation block with four parts.

- **Primary:** the ATT&CK mitigations most directly relevant to preventing or constraining the method.
- **Supporting:** hardening, detection, recovery or governance measures that reduce preconditions or impact.
- **AM-specific application:** how the mitigations apply to AM assets such as CAD files, build jobs, slicers, process recipes, feedstock, post-processing equipment, quality data and machine-control networks.
- **Residual coverage:** what ATT&CK does not cover for this method, for example physical tampering, metrology-based reverse engineering, materials science, natural disruption or commercial abuse.

Mitigations are written as `Name (Enterprise ID / ICS ID)`. Identifiers that begin with `M1` are from the Enterprise catalogue and identifiers that begin with `M0` from the ICS catalogue. A mitigation with one identifier exists in that catalogue only, or is drawn from it only. Section 9 lists every mitigation used, with a short description.

The mapping is **derived by the authors**. MITRE supplies the names, identifiers and descriptions. The assignment of mitigations to AM attack methods and the AM-specific application are this release's own analysis. A listed mitigation is relevant to the method. It is not claimed to be sufficient. Mobile mitigations are not mapped. They should be added where an AM product includes a mobile application or a mobile-controlled interface. The catalogue version and access date are given in Section 12.

## D. How to use the taxonomy: threat records

### D.1 Procedure

A threat record is produced for each asset that a product stores, transmits or processes.

1. **Asset.** Identify the asset and find its target leaf (2.x.y).
2. **Product.** Name the product with digital elements that stores, transmits, processes or executes the asset. This is the product whose manufacturer is obliged.
3. **Methods.** List the method leaves (3.x.y) that can reach the asset.
4. **Goal.** For each method, state the goal it serves (1.x).
5. **Annotations.** Copy the CIA label, the Annex I points with their strength, and the mitigations of the method leaf.

The resulting record has seven fields: asset, product engaged, method, goal, CIA, CRA anchor with strength, and mitigations. A set of such records is the AM-specific content of the risk assessment of Article 13(3). The CRA anchor answers whether and in what manner a requirement applies. The mitigations supply a vocabulary for the solutions adopted that Annex VII asks the manufacturer to describe.

### D.2 Strength of a record

1. A record starts from the label of its **method leaf**, resolved for the product named in the record. A Conditional label becomes Direct when its condition holds for that product, and outside when it does not.
2. The general limits of C.4 are applied. They can lower a record. Nothing can raise a record above the label of its method leaf.
3. Where the **target** or the **goal** of the record has a weaker label than the method, the record keeps the method's label and adds a scope note that states which part of the outcome lies outside the CRA.
4. A record whose strength differs from the label of its method leaf states the reason.

### D.3 Worked example

The *dr0wned* attack (Belikovetsky et al., 2017): an attacker gains remote access to the workstation that holds a propeller design, inserts a defect into the STL file, and the printed propeller breaks in flight.

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Asset (target) | 2.1.3 STL file of the propeller, held on the design workstation |
| Product engaged | The design software or repository through which the STL file was modified. The printer is only where the damage appears. |
| Method | 3.7.1 Cyber intrusion (entry), then 3.3.2 CAD/STL/intermediate-representation tampering |
| Goal | 1.4 Sabotage |
| CIA | Integrity |
| CRA anchor and strength | Annex I.I(2)(d), (f), (l) through Article 13(1). **Direct** for the design software or repository (label of 3.3.2, product named, attacker is not the user). |
| Scope note | The propeller is a mechanical part (2.4.1 is Conditional and its condition does not hold), so the defect in the part is outside. Its impact on health and safety is still part of the risk assessment under Article 13(2). |
| Mitigations | Code Signing (M1045 / M0945), Communication Authenticity (M0802), Restrict File and Directory Permissions (M1022 / M0922), Multi-factor Authentication (M1032 / M0932), Audit (M1047 / M0947). AM application: approval states and integrity checks across CAD, STL and conversion tools, known-good originals kept, every conversion-stage change logged. |

### D.4 Placing an observed event

The same walk works backwards. When a manufacturer observes an event in the field, it places the event by asset, method and goal. The label of the method leaf, resolved for the product, shows whether the event concerns the security of the product and may therefore engage the reporting duty of Article 14, or lies outside it.

## 1. Attack goals

Attack goals describe the attacker's intended outcome.

### 1.1 Intellectual property compromise / technical data theft `[C]`

**ID:** `AMTT-G-001` | **CRA strength:** Conditional | **Derivation:** merged  
**Source trace:** Y §4 (threat category: theft of technical data). K §4.1.1 Intellectual property compromise.

The attacker seeks to obtain, reconstruct, copy, or misuse protected AM-related knowledge.

Includes:

- theft of technical data
  - Covers unauthorised acquisition of AM technical representations or process knowledge that can enable reproduction, modification, or competitive use.
- intellectual property theft
  - Targets AM IP such as unique designs, manufacturing techniques, product innovations, trade secrets, or other protected know-how.
- CAD file theft
  - Involves unauthorised access to proprietary CAD models or design files that define the product blueprint.
- design theft
  - Covers acquisition of protected design information that can be used to duplicate, modify, or understand an AM object.
- process-knowledge theft
  - Targets manufacturing procedures, printing settings, material specifications, or production techniques that reveal how the part is made.
- trade secret theft
  - Covers confidential AM knowledge such as material compositions, post-processing methods, or unique manufacturing processes.
- counterfeiting and unlicensed reproduction
  - Uses protected or copied AM knowledge to produce fake products or replicas without the required rights or permissions.
- reverse-engineered reproduction
  - Recreates AM designs or manufacturing information from physical objects, scans, side channels, or equipment analysis rather than direct file access.
- industrial espionage
  - Involves infiltrating or compromising AM organisations to obtain proprietary information for competitive or financial advantage.
- unauthorised reproduction based on stolen or reconstructed knowledge
  - Produces AM parts from stolen or reverse-engineered knowledge without authorisation from the legitimate rights holder.

### 1.2 Unauthorised production / counterfeit / restricted-part production `[I]`

**ID:** `AMTT-G-002` | **CRA strength:** Partial | **Derivation:** kept  
**Source trace:** K §4.1.2 Unauthorised part production. Y Fig. 4 names illegal part manufacturing but excludes it from its taxonomy.

The attacker seeks to produce parts that are not authorised by the legitimate owner, regulator, or internal control regime.

Includes:

- counterfeit production
  - Produces parts that imitate genuine components and may deceive buyers or fail to meet expected quality and safety standards.
- unlicensed reproduction
  - Replicates proprietary AM parts or designs without the rights, licences, or permissions of the legitimate owner.
- grey-market production and operations
  - Creates or sells AM parts outside authorised production and distribution channels, without consent from original producers or authorised distributors.
- unauthorised overproduction
  - Produces more parts than the legitimate order, approval, or production authorisation allows.
- production of export-controlled parts
  - Uses AM to produce parts or technologies whose manufacture or transfer is restricted by export-control rules.
- production of internally restricted parts
  - Produces parts that an organisation or facility restricts because of safety, security, legal, regulatory, or policy concerns.
- evasion of supply-chain controls
  - Uses decentralised or localised AM production to avoid permitted manufacturing routes and make source tracing harder.
- bypassing regulatory or contractual constraints
  - Produces parts outside required licences, certifications, compliance steps, contractual limits, or other authorisation controls, especially in regulated sectors where safety and legal risks follow.

### 1.3 Data breach and data manipulation `[C, I]`

**ID:** `AMTT-G-003` | **CRA strength:** Direct | **Derivation:** kept  
**Source trace:** K §4.1.3 Data breach and manipulation. No goal-level counterpart in Y.

The attacker seeks unauthorised access to AM-related data or seeks to alter that data to influence production, inspection, quality, or downstream use.

Includes:

- data infiltration
  - Breaks into AM systems to access valuable data such as CAD files, STL models, slicing settings, G-Code, or confidential design knowledge.
- data exfiltration
  - Removes AM data from compromised systems, potentially exposing sensitive information over time.
- design and process information access
  - Accesses design parameters, material specifications, or printing settings to learn about quality, performance, or distinctive product features.
- manipulation of design files
  - Changes AM design files to influence the geometry, integrity, safety, or functionality of the printed object.
- manipulation of process data
  - Alters AM process-related data such as printing settings, slicing parameters, material information, or production instructions.
- manipulation of quality-control data
  - Changes inspection, testing, or acceptance data so defective or manipulated parts may appear compliant.
- manipulation of sensor readings
  - Falsifies process or environmental readings used for monitoring, control, quality assurance, or safety decisions.
- manipulation of machine instructions
  - Alters G-Code, tool paths, or equivalent machine instructions that determine how the printer executes the build.
- tampering with AM-related data or control representations
  - Broadly covers unauthorised changes to AM digital artefacts or control data that influence production or downstream use.

### 1.4 Sabotage / integrity degradation `[I, A]`

**ID:** `AMTT-G-004` | **CRA strength:** Conditional | **Derivation:** merged  
**Source trace:** Y §5 (threat category: AM sabotage). K §4.1.4 Sabotage.

The attacker seeks to reduce the integrity, reliability, safety, or functional fitness of the AM process or the resulting object.

Includes:

- reduction of mechanical strength
  - Degrades the part below required mechanical tolerance so it may fail under normal operational conditions.
- reduction of fatigue life
  - Shortens the part's useful lifetime so failure can occur prematurely during repeated or long-term use.
- introduction of hidden defects
  - Inserts flaws such as internal weaknesses, voids, or defects that may remain undetected during quality control.
- introducing weaknesses
  - Creates vulnerabilities in the part or process that reduce durability, reliability, or expected performance.
- material/property degradation
  - Alters material characteristics or part properties so the final object no longer meets its required function.
- tampering with material properties
  - Manipulates material composition, process parameters, or related conditions to change the properties of the produced part.
- contamination
  - Introduces unwanted material, foreign objects, or NBC contamination into feedstock, equipment, or parts.
- compromising part integrity
  - Undermines the final part's fit, form, function, structure, or conformance to its authorised specification.
- equipment damage
  - Harms AM mechanical, electrical, cyber, or safety-critical equipment, causing wear, malfunction, or irreparable damage.
- environmental damage
  - Causes harm beyond the part itself, including effects on nearby equipment, facility systems, customers, or deployed environments.
- disrupting production processes
  - Interferes with printers, software systems, network connectivity, or production steps to cause delays, waste, or degraded output.
- targeting critical components
  - Focuses sabotage on safety-critical or mission-critical parts where failure can cause system failures or accidents.
- concealing defects
  - Makes compromised parts appear visually or procedurally acceptable despite hidden weaknesses or structural problems.
- quality degradation that leads to unsafe or substandard output
  - Produces parts whose integrity, functionality, or safety is reduced below expected quality or performance standards.

### 1.5 Availability disruption / denial of service `[A]`

**ID:** `AMTT-G-005` | **CRA strength:** Conditional | **Derivation:** kept  
**Source trace:** K §4.1.5 Denial of service. Related in Y: §5.1.2 (cyber infrastructure, availability) and §5.1.4 (first-order effects). K's non-adversarial sub-threats are kept as exploit conditions (Section 7.2).

The attacker seeks to interrupt, delay, disable, overload, or render unavailable AM operations or supply-chain flows.

Includes:

- denial of service
  - Disrupts service availability so AM systems, equipment, production stages, or supply-chain operations cannot function as needed.
- ransomware-driven lockout
  - Locks AM control systems or related infrastructure, delaying production and creating operational or financial losses.
- production stoppage
  - Halts or interrupts AM production, causing downtime, delayed output, material waste, or recovery costs.
- logistics disruption
  - Disrupts transportation, routing, delivery, or distribution flows that AM supply-chain continuity depends on.
- equipment unavailability
  - Makes AM printers, post-processing equipment, control systems, or supporting devices unavailable for production.
- power-related disruption
  - Interrupts or destabilises power needed for AM temperature control, environmental stability, equipment operation, or safe completion of builds.
- natural disasters
  - Represents exploited disaster conditions such as floods, earthquakes, hurricanes, or wildfires that can damage AM facilities or infrastructure.
- power outages
  - Interrupt printing and supporting environmental conditions, potentially causing incomplete builds, material waste, or equipment damage.
- material contamination
  - Covers disruption conditions where AM materials absorb moisture, particulates, or other contaminants that affect material properties or part quality.
- temperature and humidity fluctuations
  - Affect AM material behaviour and process consistency, especially where powders or thermal conditions are sensitive.
- disruption of supply-chain continuity
  - Breaks the dependable flow of materials, data, equipment, parts, or services needed to sustain AM operations.

## 4. Relationship model

The three axes are connected through explicit paths:

```text
goal --goal_target--> target --target_method--> method
```

[`paths.csv`](taxonomy-data/amtt-3.0/3.0-R1/paths.csv) is the path register. A path with `path_status=approved` is a combination of goal, target and method that a source taxonomy supports. A path with `path_status=candidate` could not be resolved to one canonical node and is not asserted. The hierarchy of Sections 1 to 3 must not be expanded into a Cartesian product.

### 4.1 Source relationships

The relationships were read from the figures of the two source papers and are listed one per row in [`source-relationships.csv`](taxonomy-data/amtt-3.0/3.0-R1/authoring-audit/source-relationships.csv).

- **Y, Figures 6 and 8.** Each matrix marks a method as achievable or as partly or conditionally achievable for a target. Figure 6 (theft of technical data) has 16 marked cells and Figure 8 (AM sabotage) has 16. The cells were read from the figures rendered at 600 dpi. The matrices do not distinguish direct from indirect relationships.
- **K, Figure 3.** Lines connect goals to targets and targets to methods. The figure has 14 goal-to-target lines and 33 target-to-method lines. They were extracted from the figure image by line detection and each was checked against the image. K's text describes solid target-to-method lines as direct and a dotted line as indirect. All 33 lines in the figure are solid, so no indirect relationship is recorded. K states no feasibility.

This gives **79 source relationships**.

### 4.2 Composition of paths

1. Every source node is mapped to canonical nodes in [`source-node-mappings.csv`](taxonomy-data/amtt-3.0/3.0-R1/authoring-audit/source-node-mappings.csv), with a mapping type. `exact`: the source node and the canonical node denote the same thing. `narrower`: the source node covers part of the canonical node. `split`: the source node spans two canonical nodes.
2. **Y.** Each marked cell of Figure 6 gives a path with goal 1.1, and each marked cell of Figure 8 a path with goal 1.4, because Y defines targets and methods separately for each of its two threat categories.
3. **K.** A goal-to-target line and a target-to-method line that share the target give a path.
4. Both segments of a path come from the same source. Relationships of Y and K are never chained.
5. A path through an `exact` or `narrower` mapping is **approved**. A `narrower` mapping is carried into the path's `support_note`. A path through a `split` mapping is a **candidate** for each of the two canonical nodes, because the source does not say which part it concerns.
6. A relationship of a source node is not propagated to the canonical nodes that were split out of it. For example, a relationship of Y's *3D part specification* supports 3.3.1 and not 3.3.2 or 3.3.3.
7. Identical canonical triples are stored once. [`path-evidence.csv`](taxonomy-data/amtt-3.0/3.0-R1/authoring-audit/path-evidence.csv) lists every source relationship behind each path.
8. A combination that is absent from the register is not asserted by this release. It is not thereby impossible.

### 4.3 Coverage of release `3.0-R1`

- **52 approved paths** and **10 candidate paths**. Of the approved paths, 24 rest on Y, 26 on K and 2 on both.
- Of the 58 goals and leaves, **36** are an endpoint of an approved path, **2** occur only in candidate paths (2.2.2, 2.2.4) and **20** occur in no path.
- The leaves without a path are 2.2.5, 2.3.1, 2.3.2, 2.3.3, 2.3.4, 2.3.5, 2.3.6, 3.3.2, 3.3.3, 3.4.2, 3.4.3, 3.4.4, 3.4.5, 3.5.2, 3.5.3, 3.6.2, 3.6.3, 3.6.4, 3.6.5, 3.9.2. Most of them are splits, recasts or branches of a source node whose relationships the sources state only for the parent (rule 6). Their absence from the register says nothing about whether such attacks are possible.

Coverage describes source support for combinations. It is not a likelihood or a risk score, and it is independent of the CRA strength label.

## 5. CRA crosswalk

This section links every node to Regulation (EU) 2024/2847. The conventions, the four strength labels and the rule from which every label was derived are in Section C.3. The limits that apply to every label are in Section C.4.

Columns of the crosswalk tables:

- **Annex I requirements:** the points of Annex I the node engages. Points of Annex II and Annex VII are added where the node bears on user information or technical documentation.
- **Legal anchor:** the articles that make those points operative for the node.
- **Strength:** one of the four labels of C.3.
- **Label applies when:** for Direct, the product engaged. For Conditional, the condition. For Partial, the part of the threat that is reached. For Mostly outside CRA, where the enabling step is recorded.
- **Not covered:** what remains outside the CRA for this node.

Read by label, the boundary of the regulation is visible.

- **Direct.** Design files, intermediate models, tool paths, process parameters, sensor and quality data, AM software, firmware and connected equipment, and the methods that steal, alter or take control of them. Here Annex I applies as written, chiefly points 2(d), 2(e), 2(f) and 2(l), and Part II for equipment and third-party components.
- **Conditional.** Post-processing, replacement parts, manufactured parts, logistics of data, power behaviour and the goals that can be pursued by digital or by physical means. The CRA applies once the product engaged is identified and the condition holds.
- **Partial.** Feedstock, the production environment, physical sabotage, logistics disruption and the rights and restrictions attached to a part. Annex I reaches the data, access control and logging around the threat, not the threat itself.
- **Mostly outside CRA.** Counterfeiting as a commercial act and the exploitation of economic or emergency conditions. The CRA is not an IP, export-control, logistics, material-quality or physical-security instrument.

## 6. What was merged and what was kept distinct

### 6.1 Source nodes merged

Merged because they describe the same attack idea or asset at different levels of abstraction (merge rule 1).

| Canonical node | Y | K |
|---|---|---|
| 1.1 IP compromise / technical data theft | Theft of technical data (threat category) | Intellectual property compromise |
| 1.4 Sabotage | AM sabotage (threat category) | Sabotage |
| 2.1.1 Part specification | Part specification | 3D design and part specification |
| 2.4.1 Final manufactured part | Manufactured part | Final manufactured part |
| 2.4.2 AM equipment | AM equipment | AM equipment |
| 3.1.1 Theft | Theft | Intellectual property theft |
| 3.1.2 Reverse engineering | Reverse engineering, AM equipment analysis | Reverse engineering of AM design |
| 3.2.1 Bypassing protection | Protection removal | Bypassing digital and physical protection |
| 3.3.1 Part specification tampering | 3D part specification | Tempering with part specification (sic) |
| 3.4.1 Compromising process parameters | AM manufacturing process | Corrupting AM process parameters |
| 3.5.1 Post-processing procedure tampering | Post-processing | Tempering with post-processing procedures (sic) |
| 3.6.1 Exploitation of supply-chain weaknesses | Supply chain attack | Exploitation of supply chain weaknesses |
| 3.7.1 Cyber intrusion on AM equipment | Compromised AM equipment (cyber attacks) | Cyber intrusion on AM equipment |

Two merges need a remark.

- **3.1.2.** Y lists *AM equipment analysis* as a top-level method next to reverse engineering. It is folded into 3.1.2 as a submethod, because both reconstruct knowledge without access to the files.
- **2.2.6.** Y's *indirect manufacturing* (tooling and moulds made by AM) and K's *indirect manufacturing dependencies* (suppliers, logistics, infrastructure) share a name and the idea of a dependency of the final part, but they overlap only in part. See Section 10.

### 6.2 Concepts kept distinct

Kept apart because they describe different mechanisms or assets (merge rule 2).

- **IP compromise (1.1)** and **unauthorised production (1.2).** Obtaining knowledge is not the same as producing parts without authorisation.
- **Process specification (2.2.1)** and **process execution (2.2.2).** Recipe knowledge differs from live process control.
- **Post-processing specification (2.2.3)** and **post-processing execution (2.2.4).** Y models the specification. K's broader stage spans settings and physical execution, and is recorded as a split mapping.
- **CAD (2.1.2)**, **intermediate model (2.1.3)** and **tool path (2.1.4).** They are distinct control points in the digital thread.
- **Feedstock compromise (3.6.2)** and **equipment compromise (3.6.4, 3.7).** Both are supply-chain vectors but attack different dependencies.
- **Final manufactured part (2.4.1)** and **downstream environment (2.4.4).** The part and the harm it later causes are different target surfaces.
- **Production environment (2.4.3)** and **downstream environment (2.4.4).** K's production site and Y's environment affected through equipment or a deployed part are not equivalent.
- **Compromised equipment as an enabler (3.7.4)** and **the methods that compromise it (3.7.1 to 3.7.3).** The compromised state used as an attack route differs from the actions that create it.
- **Compromising printed parts (3.8.1)** and **the compromised part as a route (3.8.2).** K's method creates a defective part. Y's compromised part is used against its environment and is mapped to 3.8.2.
- **Overproduction (2.5.2)** and **counterfeit or restricted-part production (1.2, 3.2.2).** Overproduction can occur without any new or stolen design.
- **Exploiting supply-chain weaknesses (3.6.1)** and **disrupting logistics (3.6.6).** The first inserts something through a weak supplier. The second interrupts or diverts flows.

## 7. Source elements kept as annotations

Some source concepts are kept as annotations because they are consequences or conditions, not goals, targets or methods.

### 7.1 First-order causal effects (Y)

Y lists first-order causal effects on the target side of its sabotage taxonomy and states that they are consequences that inform the attacker's motivation. They are not nodes here.

- on the manufacturing site: availability
- on equipment: availability, lifetime
- on the manufactured part: average material use, average time per part, average part acceptance, breakage conditions

### 7.2 Exploited opportunities (K)

K distinguishes attacker goals (cyber goals, physical goals) from exploited opportunities (natural disruptions, man-made events). Exploited opportunities are conditions under which an attack becomes easier. They are not goals here. The non-adversarial sub-threats that K lists under denial of service remain as included items of goal 1.5 so that K's content is preserved, and the act of exploiting such a condition is method 3.9.2.

- natural disasters
- power outages
- material contamination by the environment
- temperature and humidity fluctuations
- labour strikes and geopolitical instability
- economic and market instability (method 3.9.1)

## 10. Limitations

1. **Not validated by practitioners.** The taxonomy is derived from the literature and has been demonstrated on one case study with two scenarios. It has not been reviewed by AM manufacturers or by a market surveillance authority.
2. **The CRA mapping is a reading, not law.** The requirement links and strength labels are the authors' analysis of the regulation. They follow one stated rule (C.3), which makes them reproducible, not authoritative. The classification of a product and the role of the company decide the actual obligations.
3. **Dependence on two sources.** The taxonomy can contain only what Y or K contain. Attacks published after K's survey, or outside the scope of both, are absent.
4. **Source figures.** The relationships were read from three figures. A reader who interprets a figure differently will obtain a different register. The readings are listed row by row in `source-relationships.csv` so that they can be checked. The section numbers in the source traces follow the editions listed in Section 12, and Y is cited from its author manuscript, whose numbering may differ from the journal layout.
5. **Node 2.2.6.** The node combines two source nodes that overlap only in part (Section 6.1). A later release may divide it.
6. **Open-ended source branches.** Y marks several branches as open-ended. Included items are therefore examples, not exhaustive lists.
7. **Splits and recasts.** 7 leaves split a source node and 4 recast source content on another axis (B.4). They add structure, not threats, but the structure is the authors' choice.
8. **ATT&CK mapping.** The assignment of mitigations to methods is derived by the authors and is tied to the catalogue version in Section 12. ATT&CK covers physical, material, legal and commercial threats only weakly, which the residual-coverage notes record.
9. **Availability.** Availability is annotated throughout, but the companion paper analyses confidentiality and integrity only.
10. **No scoring.** The taxonomy assigns no likelihood, severity or priority.

## 11. Changes in this release

Changes relative to the pre-release draft that the companion manuscript cited at first submission.

- **CRA strength labels re-derived.** The draft used free-text strength descriptions, some of which combined two levels. The rule is now stated as a decision procedure on the product engaged (C.3), every goal and leaf carries exactly one of four labels, family labels are computed from their leaves, and each row states the condition under which the label applies and what remains outside. 51 of 72 rows changed wording or level as a result. `annotations/strength-changes.csv` lists the old and the new label of every node.
- **Record-level rule added** (C.4, D.2), including the limit of Annex I.I(2)(f) to manipulation not authorised by the user.
- **Stable ID, derivation type and source trace** added to every node heading. Section references verified against the source papers.
- **Relationship register rebuilt from the source figures.** All 79 source relationships were read again from Y Figures 6 and 8 and K Figure 3, and the path register was recomposed under the rules of Section 4.2. Path IDs of the draft are not retained.
- **ATT&CK blocks compacted.** Mitigation descriptions moved to one catalogue (Section 9). No method-to-mitigation assignment was changed.
- **Usage section added** (Section D) with the threat-record procedure and a worked example.
- **Limitations, change log and citation metadata added.**
- **Machine-readable files** for nodes, relationships, mappings, paths and annotations regenerated by one build.
- **Editorial.** British spelling throughout, except official ATT&CK names. *Powder Bed Fusion* and *Directed Energy Deposition* spelled as in ISO/ASTM terminology. Duplicate items removed from the lists of 1.2, 2.2.1, 2.2.6, 2.3.1, 2.3.2, 3.1.2 and 3.6.5. One item added to 2.5.2 (production limits encoded as data). Local file paths removed from the references.
